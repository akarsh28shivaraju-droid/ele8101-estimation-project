Abstract 
This project focuses on designing and validating a vehicle state estimation system using beacon-based distance measurements and an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). The main aim is to estimate the vehicle’s position and velocity while ensuring that important physical constraints such as lane boundaries, speed limits, and one-way motion are always satisfied. 
The vehicle moves clockwise on a closed track made up of straight and curved sections with a constant lane width of 4 m. To build a reliable final estimator, the work was developed step by step through four stages: a one-dimensional linear motion model, a circular path model, a two-dimensional straight road model with lateral motion, and finally the complete track model. 
The final estimator uses Frenet-style coordinates based on along-track distance and lateral offset from the centreline. This makes it easier to apply boundary constraints and improves the robustness of the EKF. Simulation results show that the estimator accurately tracks the vehicle states, remains within all physical limits, and produces low estimation errors. RMSE results and ±3σ covariance analysis confirm that the proposed estimator is reliable and suitable for real-time implementation. 
 
1. Introduction 
State estimation is an important topic in control systems, autonomous vehicles, and robotics. In real applications, it is often impossible to measure the full state of a vehicle directly because sensors are affected by noise, limited accuracy, and cost constraints. Instead, the true vehicle states must be estimated using indirect noisy measurements. 
In this coursework, the goal is to estimate the position and velocity of a vehicle moving clockwise on a closed track using only range measurements from fixed beacons placed around the track. The vehicle must follow several physical constraints. It cannot move backward, its speed must remain below 25 m/s, and it must stay inside the lane boundaries while naturally tending to remain close to the centre of the road. 
Since the measurements are based on Euclidean distances between the vehicle and the beacons, the system becomes nonlinear. Because of this, a standard linear Kalman Filter is not suitable. Instead, the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) is used because it can handle nonlinear measurement models while still being computationally efficient enough for real-time use. 
To make the final estimator more reliable, the problem was solved progressively in four stages. The work started with a simple one-dimensional linear model, then moved to a circular path model, followed by a two-dimensional straight road model with lateral motion, and finally the full-track model. This step-by-step development helped improve understanding of the system and made the final estimator stronger and easier to justify.

4. Estimator Design 
The final estimator used in this coursework is the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). The EKF was selected because the system uses beacon-based distance measurements, which create nonlinear measurement equations. Since the vehicle sensors measure only the distance from the vehicle to each beacon, the relationship between the measurements and the vehicle position is nonlinear due to Euclidean distance calculations. 
A standard Kalman Filter works only for linear systems, so it cannot be directly applied to this problem. The EKF solves this issue by linearising the nonlinear system around the current estimated state using Jacobian matrices. 
The EKF offers a strong balance between estimation accuracy, simplicity of implementation, and computational efficiency. It is accurate enough for this problem while still being practical for real-time implementation at the required sampling rate of 100 Hz. 
Other estimators such as the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF), Particle Filter, and Moving Horizon Estimation (MHE) were also considered. However, EKF was chosen because it is easier to implement in MATLAB, requires lower computational cost, and performs very well for this level of system nonlinearity. 
The estimator was first tested using the simpler models developed in Step 1 to Step 3 before being applied to the final Step 4 full-track model.

6. Discussion 
The final estimator shows strong performance across all coursework requirements. The Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) successfully estimates the vehicle position and velocity while handling nonlinear beacon measurements and maintaining all physical constraints. 
Although the estimator performs well under nominal conditions, certain failure scenarios may affect performance. For example, if one beacon fails or provides biased measurements, the estimation accuracy may degrade due to reduced observability. Similarly, if measurement noise increases significantly or if the vehicle operates near track boundaries for extended periods, the EKF linearisation may become less accurate. These limitations highlight the importance of robust estimator design and motivate the use of fault detection or adaptive filtering techniques in real-world systems. 
One of the strongest decisions in this project was using Frenet-style coordinates (𝑠,𝑒)instead of directly estimating Cartesian coordinates (𝑥,𝑦). This made the system much easier to manage because the lane boundary condition became very simple: 
−2 ≤ 𝑒 ≤ 2 
 
Instead of solving complicated curved boundary equations, the estimator only needed to control the lateral offset from the centreline. This improved both robustness and computational efficiency. 
Another major strength was the bounded mean-reverting lateral motion model. A simple random lateral motion model would eventually allow the vehicle to drift outside the road boundaries, which would not represent realistic driving behaviour. The mean-reverting model acts like a spring that naturally pulls the vehicle back toward the centre of the lane. This better matches how real drivers behave and directly satisfies the coursework requirement. 
Beacon placement also had a major effect on estimator quality. Using three carefully positioned beacons provided strong observability across the full track while keeping the system cost low. Adding more beacons would increase cost without significant improvement, while using fewer would reduce estimation reliability. 
The ±3σ covariance analysis confirmed that the EKF tuning was appropriate. Most of the estimation errors remained inside the confidence bounds, showing that the filter is statistically consistent. This means the EKF is neither overconfident nor too conservative. 
The estimator is also suitable for real-time implementation. Since the final system only uses four states and three beacon measurements, the EKF can comfortably operate within the required 10 ms update time. 
However, some limitations still exist. The current model assumes perfect measurement timing, no missing sensor data, and no permanent beacon failures. Real systems may include delayed measurements, sensor dropouts, or constant sensor bias. 
A useful future improvement would be using an augmented-state EKF to estimate sensor bias directly. Another strong extension would be comparing EKF with UKF or MHE to test performance under stronger nonlinearities or model uncertainty. 
Even with these limitations, the final estimator is accurate, practical, and suitable for real-world implementation. 
Overall, the estimator demonstrates a strong balance between model simplicity, estimation accuracy, and computational efficiency, which is essential for real-time vehicle state estimation systems. 
 
7. Conclusion 
This coursework presented the modelling, design, and validation of an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) for estimating vehicle position and velocity using noisy beacon-based distance measurements. 
The problem was solved progressively through four stages: a one-dimensional linear model, a circular path model, a two-dimensional straight road model with lateral motion, and finally the full-track model. This step-by-step development improved understanding of the system and made the final estimator easier to justify. 
The final estimator used Frenet-style coordinates together with bounded mean-reverting lateral motion. This allowed accurate state estimation while satisfying all physical constraints, including lane boundaries, speed limits, and directional restrictions. 
Simulation results showed that the EKF accurately tracks the vehicle path, remains inside the required boundaries, and produces low estimation errors. RMSE values and ±3σ covariance consistency plots confirmed that the estimator is both mathematically reliable and practically feasible for real-time implementation. 
The final design provides a strong balance between accuracy, robustness, simplicity, and computational efficiency. The results show that the proposed estimator can realistically work in practice and successfully meets the technical requirements of the coursework. 
These results indicate that the proposed estimator is not only effective in simulation but also suitable for implementation in real-world embedded systems with limited computational resources.
